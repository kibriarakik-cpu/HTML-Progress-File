Faunal Succession, Paleontology & Taphonomy

From William Smith's canal trenches to the fossilization of life — understanding how geologists read deep time from the fossil record, and why that record is fundamentally incomplete.

Resource Type: Interactive Study Guide Organization: National Earth Olympiad (NEO) Source Material: Lecture 5 Slides — Faunal Succession Topics: Paleontology · Taphonomy · Species Problem · GTS
The Big Picture

The Core Problem This Lecture Solves

Before the 19th century, geologists faced a fundamental problem: how do you compare rock layers from different locations when you cannot physically trace them? A limestone in England might be the same age as a sandstone in France — or separated by 100 million years. There was no reliable clock. What William Smith discovered, not in a laboratory but while supervising canal excavations, changed that permanently.

This lecture builds the intellectual foundation for all biostratigraphic dating. Fossils are not merely curiosities; they are time stamps embedded in stone. But to use them reliably, you must understand how they form, what they faithfully preserve, what they distort, and what they permanently omit. The taphonomic filter is ruthless — understanding its biases is as important as understanding the fossils themselves.

Three Questions This Lecture Answers

1. How do fossils tell time? — The Principle of Faunal Succession establishes a globally reproducible sequence of life, which became the foundation of the Geologic Time Scale.

2. How do organisms become fossils? — Taphonomy describes the complete pipeline from death to discovery, revealing all the ways information is lost at each step.

3. What can we actually trust from the fossil record? — Systematic biases in preservation and the species identification problem mean the record is both incomplete and skewed toward specific environments and organisms.

Module 1

William "Strata" Smith & the Principle of Faunal Succession

The entire discipline of biostratigraphy was founded by a man who never attended university. William Smith (1769–1839), a self-educated canal engineer, derived the Principle of Faunal Succession not from abstract theorizing but from years of meticulous field observation while overseeing canal excavations across England. His story is one of the most important case studies in the history of scientific observation.

Concept 1.1 — Discovery

The Canal Surveyor Who Mapped Deep Time

The Intuitive Story First

Picture the English countryside in the 1790s. Canal projects are being cut through the landscape, and your job requires you to travel constantly, watching as workers expose fresh cross-sections of bedrock. Gradually, something becomes unmistakable: the layers always stack in the same order. Wherever your crew digs — Somerset, Yorkshire, Wales — the same sequence of limestone, shale, and sandstone appears. And each layer has its own distinctive fossil assemblage that never appears in any other layer.

Smith had the genius to realize he could invert this observation. Instead of using rock type (unreliable — limestone can form in many periods) to identify a stratum, he could use the fossil assemblage itself. A specific combination of fossil shells, belemnites, and echinoids uniquely identifies a window of time because these organisms only lived together during that specific interval. Once that interval ended, that particular combination never reappeared.

The Core Insight — Why Fossils Work as Time Stamps

Biological evolution is irreversible. Species arise, persist for a finite time window, and go extinct — permanently. That combination of species that lived together during a specific period never recombines in the same way later. Therefore, the presence of a specific fossil assemblage uniquely identifies the time interval in which it lived. This is not a working hypothesis — it is an empirically verified observation, replicated across England, across Europe, and eventually across all continents.

The Observational Process — How Smith Reasoned Step by Step

1
He observed the stacking order repeatedly. Canal cuts consistently showed rock layers in the same vertical sequence regardless of local geography. Applying the Principle of Superposition (lower layers deposited first, therefore older), this implied the layers accumulated in a fixed temporal order.
2
He catalogued fossils by stratum, not by taxonomy. Smith didn't ask "what species is this?" He asked "which layer does this always come from?" He systematically documented that specific fossils appeared reliably in specific layers, never mixed into others.
3
He tested the pattern across distances. Traveling hundreds of miles across England, Smith confirmed that the same fossil assemblages appeared at equivalent stratigraphic positions — not just locally, but regionally. The pattern held everywhere he looked.
4
He made the logical leap to correlation. If the same fossils always appear in the same relative order everywhere, then fossil assemblages can be used to correlate rock units across geographical gaps — even where you cannot physically trace the layer from one outcrop to another. Two rocks in different counties, containing the same fossil assemblage, must be the same age.
Concept 1.2 — The Principle

The Principle of Faunal Succession — Formally Stated

Formal Statement

Fossil assemblages succeed one another in a definite, recognizable, and globally reproducible order. Each assemblage is diagnostic of a specific time interval; no assemblage ever repeats in the geological record.

The principle works because evolution is irreversible. A trilobite-dominated Cambrian community, once replaced by a Devonian fish-and-coral fauna, never returns. This unidirectionality is the engine behind all biostratigraphic dating.

Olympiad Trap — Index Fossil Requirements

A good index fossil requires all three simultaneously: (1) Geographically widespread — a fossil found only in one country cannot correlate global strata; (2) Stratigraphically restricted — a species that lived for 200 Ma is useless for precision; it must have a short range to identify a narrow time window; (3) Abundant and easily identified — it must be findable and distinguishable in the field. Ammonites are the textbook example: rapidly evolving (short ranges), lived in open ocean (widespread), with distinctive coiled sutures that change between species (easily identified).

Concept 1.3 — Application

Smith's 1815 Geological Map — The Map That Changed the World

Smith synthesized decades of observation into the first large-scale geological map of any country: England, Wales, and part of Scotland, published in 1815. Approximately 2.6 metres tall and hand-coloured, it showed 23 distinct geological formations, each identified by its fossil assemblage.

What Made the Map Scientifically Revolutionary

Smith did not merely map surface outcrops. He used the consistent dip and succession of strata — deduced from fossil sequences — to predict where specific layers would be found underground. This is the hallmark of a scientific theory: predictive power. The map was a practical tool for finding coal, groundwater, and stable building stone. Smith's accompanying table of strata listed characteristic fossils for each layer and was the direct ancestor of the modern Geologic Time Scale.

Module 2

The Geologic Time Scale

The Geologic Time Scale (GTS) is geology's master temporal framework — a globally standardized division of Earth history into named intervals, each defined by its fossil content and calibrated by radiometric dating. It was assembled over two centuries by geologists working on every continent, and represents one of the greatest collaborative achievements in the history of science.

Concept 2.1 — Construction

How the Geologic Time Scale Was Built — Two Distinct Phases

1
Phase 1 — Relative ordering from faunal succession (1790s–1830s). Geologists established the relative sequence of periods using fossil assemblages alone, with no knowledge of absolute ages. Periods were named after places (Cambrian = Cambria = Wales; Devonian = Devon, England) or rock characteristics (Cretaceous = Latin for chalk). The entire global sequence rests on fossil observation — a fully independent line of evidence from any physical clock.
2
Phase 2 — Absolute calibration via radiometric dating (1900s–present). Once radioactive decay was understood, numerical ages in Ma could be assigned to boundaries already identified by faunal succession. The Cambrian base, defined by the appearance of diverse animal skeletons, is now calibrated at ~538.8 Ma.
Critical Epistemological Point — Two Independent Lines of Evidence

The relative order of geologic periods was established decades before radioactivity was discovered. When the relative ordering from fossils and the absolute ages from radioactive decay agree (and they do, with high precision), that agreement cross-validates both methods. This is what makes the GTS one of the most rigorously tested frameworks in all of science.

Module 3

Paleontology — The Study of Ancient Life

A fossil is the remains or evidence of an organism older than 10,000 years. The threshold distinguishes fossils from more recent subfossil material. Fossils fall into two fundamentally different categories that students frequently conflate: body fossils, which are actual physical remains of the organism, and trace fossils, which are evidence of the organism's activity.

Concept 3.1

Body Fossils — The Organism Itself

Body fossils are the actual physical remains of an organism — bones, shells, teeth, scales, wood, or their mineral replacements. The defining criterion is that the body itself (or a chemical transform of it) is what is preserved.

Observation Checklist — Recognizing Body Fossils in the Field

Look for: (1) Biological symmetry — bilateral (most animals), radial (echinoderms), or irregular (sponges); (2) Repeated morphological units — segments in arthropods, chambers in cephalopods, ribs in bivalves; (3) Non-fractal geometry — biological growth produces smooth curves and regular forms unlike mineral crystal faces or random fractures; (4) Distinctive mineral texture or porosity — fossil shells often differ in lustre or microstructure from the surrounding matrix.

Compression Preservation (e.g., 410 Ma arthropod in shale)

  • Organism buried in fine mud and flattened by compaction
  • Volatile organics driven off; may leave a carbon film
  • Fine laminae preserve delicate appendages
  • Best for thin, flat organisms in anoxic, fine-grained settings

Amber Preservation (e.g., 25 Ma arthropod)

  • Organism trapped in tree resin and enveloped
  • Hardened resin = amber; creates anoxic, antiseptic microenvironment
  • 3D structure often perfectly preserved, including soft tissue and wing venation
  • Fragmented DNA can occasionally survive for tens of millions of years
Concept 3.2

Trace Fossils — Evidence of Behavior

Trace fossils (or ichnofossils) preserve the activity of organisms — not the organisms themselves. They are the geological equivalent of forensic evidence: a burrow tells you something was here and doing something, even if the organism's body was never preserved. Soft-bodied organisms that leave no body fossils routinely leave burrows and tracks, making ichnology the primary evidence for complex animal behavior before the Cambrian Explosion.

Why Trace Fossils Are Scientifically Powerful

Trace fossils record behavior in life, not condition at death. A burrow proves the animal was alive and actively processing sediment. Body fossils can be transported far from where the organism lived; trace fossils are almost always in situ — exactly where the organism was. Ichnoassemblages (groups of trace fossils found together) are reliable indicators of paleoenvironment: the Skolithos ichnofacies (vertical burrows by suspension feeders) indicates high-energy shoreface conditions, while the Zoophycos ichnofacies indicates deep, quiet, oxygen-limited environments.

Module 4

Taphonomy — The Study of the Fossilization Process

Taphonomy (from Greek taphos = burial, nomos = law) encompasses every process that occurs between an organism's death and its eventual discovery as a fossil — or its complete destruction along the way. The fundamental taphonomic reality is captured in the phrase from the slides: "It's difficult to become a fossil."

Concept 4.1 — The Pipeline

From Life to Collection — The Full Taphonomic Pipeline

Each stage acts as a filter that removes information. By the time a fossil reaches a scientist's hands, it represents only a tiny fraction of the original biological community. Click each stage below to see what happens at that step:

🌿
Life Assemblage
The complete, living biological community
▶

Every organism alive in a given environment — the true biological diversity of the time and place. Includes all taxa equally: hard-shelled and entirely soft-bodied, juveniles and adults. This is the benchmark against which all preservation is measured, and it is the assemblage that almost never survives intact into the rock record.

↓
First filter: soft-bodied organisms begin decaying within hours of death
💀
Stage 1 — Necrolysis (Pre-burial Decay)
Organic matter attacked by oxygen, bacteria, and fungi
▶

Immediately after death, microbial decomposition begins. Soft tissues break down rapidly in the presence of oxygen. Hard parts — shells, bones, teeth — may resist. Decay retardants are the key to preservation:

  • Anoxia — in oxygen-depleted environments (deep basins, swamp bottoms), aerobic decomposers cannot function and decay slows dramatically
  • Rapid burial — sudden entombment in sediment cuts off oxygen and removes the organism from surface scavengers
  • Freezing — temperatures below 0°C halt virtually all microbial activity

Classic examples from the slides: A juvenile mammoth (40,000 yr, Siberia) with flesh, skin, and hair intact — preserved by permafrost. An Incan child mummy (500 yr, Andes) — preserved by glacial ice. Both demonstrate how freezing, as a decay retardant, preserves soft tissue for timescales vastly longer than normally possible.

↓
Second filter: physical, biological, and chemical destruction before burial
🌊
Stage 2 — Biostratinomy (Pre-burial Alteration)
All processes acting on remains at or near the surface before burial
▶

Three categories of destruction operate on remains at the surface before they are buried:

  • A. Mechanical processes — abrasion and transport by water currents or wind. A clam shell tumbling along a beach progressively loses its ribs, ornamentation , and eventually its taxonomic identity, becoming an unidentifiable white pebble. The slide example of progressive abrasion of clam shells illustrates this directly. Retardants: rapid burial; low current velocity.
  • B. Biological processes — predation, scavenging (breaks bones into fragments), trampling, and boring. The slide example shows clionid sponges drilling tiny holes into bivalve shells — weakening them and, paradoxically, creating trace fossils in the same shell that was being destroyed as a potential body fossil.
  • C. Chemical processes — slightly acidic groundwater or rainwater dissolves calcium carbonate shells and bones. Freshwater environments (low pH) are particularly destructive, which is why freshwater fossil assemblages are typically impoverished relative to marine ones.
↓
Third filter: post-burial chemical and physical alteration
⚗️
Stage 3 — Diagenesis (Post-burial Alteration)
Compaction, cementation, recrystallization, and replacement after burial
▶

After burial, increasing temperature, pressure, and chemically active pore fluids drive further changes. The Total Fossil Assemblage is whatever survives diagenesis — significantly reduced from the life assemblage.

  • Compaction — the weight of overlying sediment crushes and flattens 3D fossils into 2D impressions
  • Recrystallization — metastable minerals (e.g., aragonite shells) reorganize into stable forms (calcite), preserving shape but erasing original geochemistry
  • Replacement — original minerals are substituted molecule-by-molecule by new minerals from groundwater
  • Metamorphism — if burial is deep enough, fossils are destroyed entirely; metamorphic rocks rarely contain recognizable fossils
↓
Fourth filter: only accessible, surface-exposed fossils can be discovered
🔍
Collection — The Scientific Record
What scientists actually find and study
▶

The final filter: fossils must be exposed at the surface by erosion, physically accessible, and large enough to be noticed. Most of the subsurface and deep-sea fossil record is permanently inaccessible.

  • Geographic bias — Western Europe and North America are overrepresented in published literature relative to their actual fossil diversity
  • Size bias — macrofossils collected far more readily than microfossils; most Precambrian life was microscopic and severely underrepresented
  • Environment bias — marine, carbonate-shelled, shallow-water organisms dominate the entire record
  • The result — >250,000 named fossil species across 3.8 billion years vs. ~1.9 million named living species; billions of ancient species likely never preserved at all
Systematic Biases of the Fossil Record

The record is biased toward: (1) marine organisms (marine sediments accumulate more continuously); (2) hard-part-bearing organisms — shells and bones over soft tissue; (3) rapidly subsiding basins where burial is fast; (4) shallow-water environments. Any conclusion from absence in the fossil record must account for these biases — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence unless taphonomic failure can be ruled out.

Module 5

Fossilization Modes — Five Ways Life Becomes Stone

Once an organism's remains survive taphonomic filtering and are buried, the specific chemistry and physics of the burial environment determine the mode of fossilization. Each mode produces a structurally and geochemically distinct fossil type, with different implications for what information is preserved and what is irrecoverably lost. Select a mode below to explore:

🌿
A. Carbonization
Compression and heat drive off volatile elements, leaving a carbon film

When an organism is buried under sediment and subjected to progressively increasing heat and pressure, volatile elements — nitrogen, hydrogen, and oxygen — are driven off as gases. What remains is a thin, two-dimensional film of nearly pure carbon that faithfully preserves the organism's outline and often its finest anatomical details.

1
Burial under reducing (anoxic) conditions — essential for limiting microbial decay of organic matter before compression drives volatile expulsion.
2
Volatilization of H, N, O — driven by increasing temperature with burial depth. The residual carbon is chemically stable and resistant to further alteration.
3
Carbon film preserved — a dark, flat imprint on a lighter sediment matrix, often with fin rays, leaf veins, or bark texture preserved at sub-millimetre resolution.
Common Organisms and Geological Significance

Plants (fern fronds, leaves, bark) and fish are most commonly carbonized. Coal itself is the extreme product of carbonization applied to entire swamp ecosystems: peat → lignite → bituminous coal → anthracite represents a progressive carbonization series with increasing burial maturity. The slides show a carbonized fish and a carbonized fern — both appearing as dark silhouettes against a pale rock matrix.

🐚
B. Molds and Casts
Impressions in enclosing sediment; voids later filled with new mineral

When a shell or bone is buried in sediment and subsequently dissolves because percolating groundwater is slightly acidic, it leaves behind a void in the surrounding rock. This void is itself a fossil — it perfectly records the shape of the original object. If the void is later filled by a new mineral precipitating from groundwater, the resulting solid object is a cast. The slide uses the sand bucket analogy with precision: the bucket is the mold (the negative space that records the shape); the sandcastle you produce is the cast (the positive replica).

External Mold

  • Records the exterior surface of the organism
  • Preserves external ornamentation: ribs, ridges, spines, growth lines
  • A concave impression in the surrounding matrix

Internal Mold (Steinkern)

  • Forms when sediment fills a shell interior, then the shell dissolves
  • Preserves internal morphology: muscle scars, columella detail
  • Steinkern = German for "stone core"
🪵
C. Permineralization ("Petrification")
Minerals crystallize in pore spaces; original material often remains in cell walls

Permineralization is the most common mode of fossilization for organisms with porous hard parts — bone, wood, sponge, and coral. Mineral-rich groundwater migrates through the network of pore spaces and channels within the buried organism's structure. As it moves, minerals precipitate and crystallize within the voids, turning the organism to stone from the inside out while the original structural material often remains intact in the walls.

Critical Distinction — Permineralization vs. Replacement

In permineralization, the original organic or mineral material remains in the cell walls — only the pore spaces are filled with new mineral. In replacement (Mode E), the original material is removed and substituted entirely. Think of permineralization as filling an empty cupboard with cement (the cupboard wood remains) versus replacement as rebuilding the entire cupboard from different wood entirely.

Common infilling minerals: silica (SiO₂) — produces hard, glassy petrified wood; calcite (CaCO₃) — common in marine organisms; pyrite (FeS₂) — in anoxic, sulfur-rich burial environments. The extraordinary example from the slides: permineralized wood at 500× magnification preserves intact cell walls and cell lumens — microscopic anatomy intact over hundreds of millions of years.

💎
D. Recrystallization
Crystal structure reorganizes to a more stable polymorph; chemical identity unchanged

Recrystallization occurs when the mineral identity of a shell remains the same — the chemical formula is unchanged — but its crystal structure reorganizes into a thermodynamically more stable form. The overall shape and surface morphology of the original organism are preserved; the internal crystal lattice and original geochemistry are not.

The Classic Example: Aragonite → Calcite

Many marine invertebrates (bivalves, gastropods, corals) secrete shells in aragonite — the metastable polymorph of CaCO₃ (orthorhombic crystal system). Aragonite is unstable on geological timescales. Under burial and pore fluid influence, aragonite slowly recrystallizes to calcite — the stable polymorph of CaCO₃ (trigonal system). Both share the formula CaCO₃; only the crystal structure and density differ. The critical implication: recrystallized shells look intact but are geochemically altered. Stable isotope ratios (δ¹⁸O, δ¹³C) used for paleotemperature reconstruction are erased. This is a major methodological trap in paleoclimatology.

⚗️
E. Replacement
Original mineral completely substituted by a chemically different mineral

Replacement is the most chemically radical mode. Unlike recrystallization (same formula, different crystal structure) or permineralization (voids filled, original material remains), replacement removes the original material molecule-by-molecule and substitutes it with an entirely different mineral. The shape and fine detail of the original organism can be beautifully preserved even as its entire chemistry is transformed.

Pyritization — The Most Spectacular Replacement

Pyrite (FeS₂) replacement occurs under anoxic, sulfur-rich conditions where sulfate-reducing bacteria produce H₂S, which reacts with iron to precipitate pyrite. This replaces original calcite or chitin with glittering metallic pyrite. The slides show: a brachiopod with calcite replaced by pyrite, and a trilobite with calcite and chitin replaced by pyrite — golden metallic fossils with extraordinary morphological detail, sometimes called "fool's gold fossils." Pyritization is common in black shales and other anoxic depositional environments.

"It's Difficult to Become a Fossil" — The Incomplete Record

Given everything that must go right — hard parts, rapid burial, avoiding mechanical, chemical, and biological destruction, surviving diagenesis, being exposed by erosion, and being found by a scientist — the numbers speak for themselves:

Typical Fossilization Requirements — The Three-Point Checklist

For preservation, an organism generally needs: (1) Hard parts — shell, bone, or teeth; soft tissue almost never survives without extraordinary conditions; (2) Rapid burial — minimizes time in the destructive biostratinomy zone and exposure to scavengers, abrasion, and dissolution; (3) Avoidance of physical, chemical, and biological alteration — both before and after burial. An organism checking all three boxes still faces diagenetic alteration and may never be exposed at the surface where a scientist can find it.

Module 6

The Species Problem in Paleontology

Even after successfully extracting a fossil from rock, the paleontologist faces a second, subtler challenge: determining what species it represents. This is far more difficult than it sounds, because the standard biological definition of a species — built on the concept of interbreeding — is completely inapplicable to organisms that have been dead for millions of years. This is the species problem in paleontology, and it has profound consequences for how we interpret fossil diversity.

Concept 6.1 — Taxonomy

The Linnaean Taxonomic Scheme

All organisms — living and fossil — are classified within the hierarchical system established by Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century. The system groups organisms by shared characteristics, from the broadest grouping (Domain) to the most specific (Species). Every level is nested within the one above it.

Binomial Nomenclature — The Rules

Every species receives a two-part Latin name: Genus species. Always italicized. Genus is capitalized; species epithet is lowercase. Example: Homo sapiens (genus Homo, epithet sapiens = "wise"). When abbreviated after first use: H. sapiens. The full genus name is required because species epithets are not unique — many genera may have a species called vulgaris or major.

Concept 6.2

The Biological Species Concept

The standard definition used in modern biology is the biological species concept (Ernst Mayr, 1942):

Formal Definition

"A species is a group of morphologically similar individuals that can interbreed and produce viable, fertile offspring."

The key criterion is reproductive isolation — members of different species cannot successfully interbreed, or if they do, their offspring are infertile (e.g., a mule from a horse and donkey). This definition works elegantly for living organisms that can be observed. It is completely inapplicable to fossils, since you cannot watch a 400-million-year-old trilobite breed.

Paleontologists must therefore rely solely on morphology (shape, size, structure) to identify fossil species. But morphological variation in a fossil population may not reflect genuine species-level differences at all — it may reflect three other phenomena entirely.

Concept 6.3 — Sources of Confusion

Three Sources of Morphological Confusion — Interactive

Because paleontologists cannot test breeding compatibility, morphological variation is their only tool. But the same fossil population may show wide morphological variation for reasons having nothing to do with species boundaries. Select each source of confusion below to understand the distinction:

a. Ontogeny — Juvenile vs. Adult Morphology

Ontogeny is the developmental history of an individual organism from juvenile to adult. In many invertebrates, juveniles look dramatically different from adults of the same species — different proportions, fewer segments, absent or simplified ornamentation. A naive observer collecting both from the same bed could classify them as two entirely separate species.

The slide example shows trilobite ontogeny: the earliest stage (protaspid) is a tiny, simple disc with no thoracic segments; intermediate stages (meraspid) add segments progressively; the adult (holaspid) has the full complement of thoracic segments and complete pygidium. All stages belong to the same species. The strategy for distinguishing ontogeny from true species: collect abundant specimens from the same bed, perform statistical analysis of morphological variation, and compare to growth series of related living arthropods.

b. Sexual Dimorphism — Males vs. Females

Sexual dimorphism occurs when males and females of the same species differ significantly in size, shape, or ornamentation. The slide example shows two crabs — one with a dramatically enlarged claw. If only one sex were preserved in a fossil assemblage, it might be described as a different species from fossils representing the other sex found at a different locality.

A famous paleontological example: ammonites exhibit well-documented dimorphism. Large-bodied macroconchs (probably females) and small-bodied microconchs (probably males) were for decades described as separate genera and species. Statistical detection of dimorphism requires recognizing a bimodal size-frequency distribution in a single bed, identical stratigraphic ranges for both morphs, and morphological intergrades linking them.

c. Ecophenetic Variability — Same Species, Different Environment

Ecophenetic variation occurs when individuals of the same species develop different morphologies in response to different environmental conditions — water flow, temperature, light, substrate type. This is phenotypic plasticity driven by the environment, not by genetic differentiation. Two populations of the same species living in different environments may look as different as two separate species.

The slide example shows Acropora humilis coral: in high-wave-energy environments, colonies are compact, robust, and short-branched; in calm, protected waters, the same species produces elongated, delicate, thin branches. A fossil of each would be unrecognizable as the same species without environmental context. The strategy: studying geographic and ecological gradients in modern relatives; using geochemical proxies (trace elements, stable isotopes) to reconstruct the original environment of each fossil population.

Strategies for Dealing with the Species Problem

a
Characterize morphology statistically from abundant, well-preserved fossils. Cluster analysis and multivariate morphometrics can distinguish genuine species-level separation from individual or ontogenetic variation. Taxonomic conclusions from single or few specimens are unreliable and should always be flagged as preliminary.
b
Integrate fossil data with modern organism data. If a living relative shows a specific degree of ontogenetic or ecophenetic variation, that variation provides a template for interpreting fossil variation. This is the principle of uniformitarianism applied to biological morphology — the same developmental and ecological processes that operate today operated in the past.

Complete Glossary — Lecture 5 Key Terms

All bolded terms from Lecture 5, defined precisely as introduced — always grounded in observational evidence.

BiostratigraphyThe use of fossil assemblages to establish relative age and correlate rock units across geographical gaps.
BiostratinomyAll physical, biological, and chemical processes destroying or altering organic remains between death and burial — Stage 2 of taphonomy.
Biozone / Range zoneA stratigraphic interval defined by the occurrence of a specific taxon; the formal unit of biostratigraphic classification.
Body fossilA fossil consisting of actual physical remains — bones, shells, teeth — or their mineral replacements. Distinct from trace fossils.
CarbonizationFossilization mode in which burial, compression, and heat drive off volatile elements (N, H, O), leaving a thin pure-carbon film of the organism.
CastA solid fossil formed when a void (mold) left by a dissolved organism is filled by a new mineral precipitating from groundwater.
CoproliteFossilized feces; a trace fossil recording diet, digestive physiology, and the presence of an organism even without body fossil evidence.
DiagenesisAll post-burial physical and chemical changes to sediment and fossils — compaction, cementation, recrystallization, and replacement.
Ecophenetic variabilityMorphological differences within a single species driven by different environmental conditions rather than genetic differentiation.
FossilThe remains or evidence of ancient organisms older than 10,000 years, preserved in rock or sediment.
Geologic Time Scale (GTS)The globally standardized hierarchy of time units (Eons, Eras, Periods, Epochs) defined by faunal succession and calibrated by radiometric dating.
IchnofaciesA characteristic association of trace fossils diagnostic of a particular paleoenvironment (e.g., Skolithos ichnofacies = high-energy shoreface).
Index fossilA fossil from a geographically widespread, stratigraphically short-ranged, easily identified species — the ideal biostratigraphic time stamp.
Linnaean taxonomyThe hierarchical classification system (Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species) devised by Carl Linnaeus.
MoldAn impression — the negative space left in surrounding rock after an organism's shell or bone has dissolved.
NecrolysisPre-burial decay; the attack of organic matter by oxygen, bacteria, and fungi immediately following death — Stage 1 of taphonomy.
OntogenyThe developmental history of an individual from juvenile to adult; a source of morphological variation that can mimic species-level differences in fossils.
PaleontologyThe scientific study of ancient life through fossil evidence — body fossils, trace fossils, and their geological context.
PermineralizationFossilization mode in which minerals precipitate from groundwater into pore spaces within buried hard parts, while original structural material often remains intact in walls.
Principle of Faunal SuccessionFossil assemblages succeed one another in a definite, globally reproducible sequence; the same assemblage always occupies the same relative stratigraphic position.
PyritizationReplacement of original shell or chitin by iron sulfide (FeS₂) under anoxic, sulfur-rich burial conditions; produces metallic "fool's gold" fossils.
RecrystallizationMineral change where the chemical formula stays the same (e.g., CaCO₃) but crystal structure reorganizes to a more stable polymorph (e.g., aragonite → calcite).
ReplacementMolecule-by-molecule substitution of the original mineral with an entirely different mineral — shape is preserved, original chemistry is destroyed.
Sexual dimorphismMorphological differences between males and females of the same species; commonly misidentified as separate species in fossil collections.
Species (biological concept)Morphologically similar individuals capable of interbreeding and producing viable, fertile offspring — not directly testable on fossil taxa.
TaphonomyThe study of all processes between an organism's death and its discovery as a fossil — from decay through burial, diagenesis, and eventual exposure.
Trace fossil (ichnofossil)Preserved evidence of organism activity — burrows, footprints, borings, coprolites — rather than remains of the organism itself.
William SmithSelf-educated English geologist (1769–1839) who established the Principle of Faunal Succession and produced the first geological map of England, Wales, and Scotland (1815).
